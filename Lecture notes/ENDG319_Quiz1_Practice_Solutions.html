Problem 1

Events, overlap, and reliability

During a test, let \(A\) be the event that a temperature alarm activates and \(B\) the event that a vibration alarm activates. The model gives \(P(A)=0.30\), \(P(B)=0.40\), and \(P(A\cup B)=0.58\).

(a) Find the probability that both alarms activate and the probability that exactly one alarm activates.

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · inclusion–exclusion
\[P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P(A\cap B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cup B)\]
Substitute
\[P(A\cap B)=0.30+0.40-0.58=\mathbf{0.12}\]

Do not assume independence to get the intersection. Independence is not given, and part (b) asks you to check it. The union is given precisely so the intersection can be found without any assumption.

Formula · exactly one
\[P(\text{exactly one})=P(A\cup B)-P(A\cap B)=P(A)+P(B)-2P(A\cap B)\]
Substitute
\[P(\text{exactly one})=0.58-0.12=\mathbf{0.46}\]

The double count: \(P(A)+P(B)\) includes the overlap twice. Removing it once gives the union; removing it twice leaves only the "exactly one" regions.

(b) Find \(P(A\mid B)\). Are \(A\) and \(B\) independent? Are they mutually exclusive? Justify both conclusions numerically.

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · conditional probability
\[P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)},\qquad P(B)>0\]
Substitute
\[P(A\mid B)=\frac{0.12}{0.40}=\mathbf{0.30}=P(A)\]
Definitions
\[\text{Independent: }P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B)\qquad \text{Mutually exclusive: }P(A\cap B)=0\]
Check
\[P(A)P(B)=(0.30)(0.40)=0.12=P(A\cap B)\ \Rightarrow\ \text{independent}\] \[P(A\cap B)=0.12>0\ \Rightarrow\ \text{not mutually exclusive}\]

Knowing that \(B\) occurred does not change the probability of \(A\). Independence and mutual exclusivity are different properties. Two events with positive probability that are mutually exclusive are strongly dependent, because one occurring rules the other out.

(c) In a separate configuration, three alarm modules activate independently, each with probability 0.30. Find the probability that at least one module activates.

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · complement + independence
\[P(\text{at least one})=1-P(\text{none})=1-\prod_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(1-p_i\bigr)\]
Substitute
\[P(\text{at least one})=1-(1-0.30)^3=1-0.343=\mathbf{0.6570}\]

The probability is 65.70%. It is not \(3(0.30)=0.90\), because that sum counts outcomes where two or three modules activate more than once. With four modules the sum would reach 1.20, which shows the method is wrong.

Problem 2

Sampling choices and interpreting summaries

A shipment contains 1,200 sensors. To study response times, an engineer tests the 40 sensors in the most easily reached tray rather than selecting sensors throughout the shipment. The density histogram of the 40 recorded response times has the following bar heights. All times are less than 10 seconds.

Response time, \(t\) (s)Class width (s)Density height (s⁻¹)
\(0\le t<2\)20.150
\(2\le t<5\)30.100
\(5\le t<10\)50.080

(a) Identify the sampling method. Would testing 400 sensors chosen in the same convenient way necessarily eliminate selection bias? Briefly describe a better sampling procedure.

Show solutionHide solution

This is a convenience sample, not a simple random sample. The accessible sensors may differ systematically from the rest of the shipment, for example if that tray came from one production batch.

A larger sample collected the same way does not necessarily remove the bias. Bias comes from how units are selected, not from how many. Increasing \(n\) only makes the estimate more precise around the wrong value.

Better: number all 1,200 sensors and randomly select 40 distinct identifiers, so every 40-sensor subset has the same chance of selection (a simple random sample).

(b) Use the histogram areas to verify that the total relative frequency is 1. Find the proportion of recorded times below 5 s and the number of recorded times that are at least 5 s.

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · density histogram
\[\text{relative frequency of a class}=\text{width}\times\text{density height},\qquad \sum_{\text{classes}}\text{width}\times\text{height}=1\] \[\text{frequency (count)}=n\times\text{relative frequency}\]
Substitute
\[2(0.150)+3(0.100)+5(0.080)=0.30+0.30+0.40=1\] \[P(t<5)=0.30+0.30=\mathbf{0.60}\qquad \#(t\ge 5)=40(0.40)=\mathbf{16}\text{ sensors}\]

These summaries describe the recorded sample. Because of part (a), they do not establish the same proportions for the whole shipment.

(c) A technician says, "The first bar is tallest, so the first class contains the most sensors." Is this correct? Explain without redrawing the histogram.

Show solutionHide solution
Principle
\[\text{count in class}=n\times(\text{width}\times\text{height})\quad\text{(area, not height)}\]
Substitute
\[40(2\times0.150)=12,\qquad 40(3\times0.100)=12,\qquad 40(5\times0.080)=16\]

No. With unequal class widths, compare areas, not heights. The first class has the highest density (sensors per second of width), but the third class has the largest frequency.

(d) For an error-sensitivity check, replace the largest recorded value, 9.0 s, by 90.0 s while leaving the other 39 values unchanged. By how much does the sample mean change? Does the sample median change?

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · effect of changing one value on the mean
\[\bar{x}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta\bar{x}=\frac{x_{\text{new}}-x_{\text{old}}}{n}\]
Substitute
\[\Delta\bar{x}=\frac{90.0-9.0}{40}=\mathbf{2.025\ s}\]
Formula · median for even n
\[\tilde{x}=\tfrac12\bigl(x_{(n/2)}+x_{(n/2+1)}\bigr)=\tfrac12\bigl(x_{(20)}+x_{(21)}\bigr)\]

The median is unchanged. The altered observation was the largest and remains the largest, so the 20th and 21st ordered values do not move.

This shows the mean is sensitive to extreme values and the median is resistant. It does not justify automatically removing a genuine extreme observation.

Problem 3

Recovering a distribution from its CDF

Let \(X\) be a discrete delay, in minutes, in a data-transfer process. Its cumulative distribution function \(F(x)=P(X\le x)\) is specified for every real threshold \(x\):

Threshold interval\(F(x)\)
\(x<0\)0
\(0\le x<2\)0.20
\(2\le x<5\)0.70
\(x\ge5\)1.00

(a) Identify the possible values of \(X\) and recover its probability mass function. Explain how each probability is obtained from the CDF.

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · PMF from CDF (jump size)
\[p(x)=P(X=x)=F(x)-F(x^-)\qquad\text{where }F(x^-)=\lim_{t\uparrow x}F(t)\]

Each jump in \(F\) marks a possible value, and the jump height is that value's probability. The jumps are at 0, 2 and 5 minutes.

Substitute
\[p(0)=0.20-0=\mathbf{0.20},\quad p(2)=0.70-0.20=\mathbf{0.50},\quad p(5)=1.00-0.70=\mathbf{0.30}\] \[p(x)=0\text{ otherwise};\qquad 0.20+0.50+0.30=1\]

(b) Find \(P(2\le X<5)\) and \(P(2<X\le5)\). Explain why changing which endpoint is included changes the answer.

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · interval probabilities for a discrete variable
\[P(a<X\le b)=F(b)-F(a),\qquad P(a\le X<b)=F(b^-)-F(a^-)\] \[\text{equivalently, add }p(x)\text{ over the possible values inside the interval}\]
Substitute
\[P(2\le X<5)=F(5^-)-F(2^-)=0.70-0.20=P(X=2)=\mathbf{0.50}\] \[P(2<X\le5)=F(5)-F(2)=1.00-0.70=P(X=5)=\mathbf{0.30}\]

Both endpoints carry positive probability mass. The first interval contains only the value 2; the second contains only 5. For a continuous variable the two answers would match, because single points have probability zero. For a discrete variable, the endpoint convention matters.

(c) Calculate \(E[X]\), \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\), and the standard deviation of \(X\).

Show solutionHide solution
Formulas
\[E[X]=\sum_x x\,p(x),\qquad E[X^2]=\sum_x x^2\,p(x)\] \[\mathrm{Var}(X)=E[X^2]-\bigl(E[X]\bigr)^2,\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}\]
Substitute
\[E[X]=0(0.20)+2(0.50)+5(0.30)=\mathbf{2.50\ min}\] \[E[X^2]=0^2(0.20)+2^2(0.50)+5^2(0.30)=9.50\ \text{min}^2\] \[\mathrm{Var}(X)=9.50-2.50^2=\mathbf{3.25\ min^2},\qquad \sigma=\sqrt{3.25}\approx\mathbf{1.8028\ min}\]

The model probabilities are the weights. This is a population (model) variance, so there is no sample-variance \(n-1\) divisor.

(d) Must the expected delay be one of the possible observed delays? Explain using this distribution.

Show solutionHide solution

No. The expected value is a probability-weighted long-run average, not a likely outcome. Here \(E[X]=2.50\) min, but an individual delay can only be 0, 2 or 5 min.

Problem 4

Planning a set of engineering tests

A laboratory has nine distinct test protocols: four thermal protocols and five vibration protocols. An engineer will use three different protocols, each at most once.

(a) How many ordered schedules of three protocols are possible? Explain why \(9^3\) is not the correct count.

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · permutations (ordered, no repetition)
\[P(n,r)=n(n-1)\cdots(n-r+1)=\frac{n!}{(n-r)!}\]
Substitute
\[P(9,3)=9\times8\times7=\frac{9!}{6!}=\mathbf{504}\]

The three positions are distinguishable and a protocol cannot repeat. The count \(9^3=729\) would allow the same protocol in more than one position, such as protocol 4 three times.

(b) An acceptable test plan is an unordered group of three protocols containing at least one thermal protocol. How many acceptable plans are there?

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · combinations and the complement
\[C(n,r)=\binom{n}{r}=\frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}\] \[\#(\text{at least one thermal})=\#(\text{all groups})-\#(\text{no thermal})\]
Substitute
\[C(9,3)-C(5,3)=84-10=\mathbf{74}\]
Check · disjoint cases
\[C(4,1)C(5,2)+C(4,2)C(5,1)+C(4,3)=40+30+4=74\]
Common error: \(C(4,1)\,C(8,2)=112\). Choosing a "guaranteed" thermal protocol first and then any two others counts each plan with 2 thermal protocols twice and each plan with 3 thermal protocols three times: \(40+2(30)+3(4)=112\).

Changing the order of a plan does not create a new plan, which is why combinations are used here.

(c) One plan is selected uniformly from the acceptable plans in part (b). What is the probability that the selected plan contains exactly two thermal protocols? State the reference set used in your denominator.

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · equally likely outcomes
\[P(E)=\frac{\#\text{ outcomes in }E}{\#\text{ outcomes in the reference set}}\]
Substitute
\[\#(\text{exactly two thermal})=C(4,2)\,C(5,1)=6\times5=30\] \[P(\text{exactly two thermal}\mid\text{acceptable})=\frac{30}{74}=\frac{15}{37}\approx\mathbf{0.4054}\]

The reference set is the 74 acceptable plans, not all 84 three-protocol groups and not the 504 ordered schedules. Those denominators describe different random experiments.

Problem 5

A joint density on a triangular region

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be two dimensionless process measurements with joint probability density

\[f(x,y)=\begin{cases}2, & 0<y<x<1\\ 0, & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\]

(a) Sketch the support and shade the event \(Y>X/2\) within that support. Set up and evaluate the corresponding probability integral.

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · probability of a region
\[P\bigl((X,Y)\in R\bigr)=\iint_R f(x,y)\,dy\,dx\]

For a fixed \(x\in(0,1)\), the event requires \(x/2<y<x\).

Substitute
\[P(Y>X/2)=\int_0^1\!\int_{x/2}^{x}2\,dy\,dx=\int_0^1 2\Bigl(x-\frac{x}{2}\Bigr)dx=\int_0^1 x\,dx=\mathbf{\tfrac12}\]

Geometric check: with a constant density, probability = density × area. The shaded triangle has base 0.5 (along x = 1) and height 1, so its area is ¼, and 2 × ¼ = ½.

(b) Find the two marginal densities \(f_X(x)\) and \(f_Y(y)\), including their support and zero values elsewhere.

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · marginal densities
\[f_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dy,\qquad f_Y(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x,y)\,dx\]

The limits come from the support, not from 0 to 1. Fix one variable and read off the range of the other along the slice shown below.

Substitute
\[f_X(x)=\int_0^{x}2\,dy=\mathbf{2x},\quad 0<x<1;\quad 0\text{ otherwise}\] \[f_Y(y)=\int_y^{1}2\,dx=\mathbf{2(1-y)},\quad 0<y<1;\quad 0\text{ otherwise}\]
Check
\[\int_0^1 2x\,dx=1,\qquad \int_0^1 2(1-y)\,dy=1\]

Values assigned at boundary points do not affect probabilities.

(c) Find \(P(Y<0.20\mid X=0.60)\). Compare it with the unconditional probability \(P(Y<0.20)\), and use the comparison to assess independence.

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · conditional density
\[f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)=\frac{f(x,y)}{f_X(x)},\quad f_X(x)>0;\qquad P(Y<c\mid X=x)=\int_{-\infty}^{c}f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x)\,dy\]
Substitute
\[f_{Y\mid X}(y\mid 0.60)=\frac{2}{2(0.60)}=\frac{5}{3},\quad 0<y<0.60\] \[P(Y<0.20\mid X=0.60)=\int_0^{0.20}\frac53\,dy=\frac13\approx\mathbf{0.3333}\]
Formula · unconditional probability from the marginal
\[P(Y<c)=\int_{-\infty}^{c}f_Y(y)\,dy\]
Substitute
\[P(Y<0.20)=\int_0^{0.20}2(1-y)\,dy=\Bigl[2y-y^2\Bigr]_0^{0.20}=0.40-0.04=\mathbf{0.36}\]

Since \(\tfrac13\ne0.36\), X and Y are dependent. More generally, \(P(Y<0.20\mid X=x)=0.20/x\) for \(0.20<x<1\), which changes with \(x\). The support also shows this: it is not a rectangle, so knowing \(X\) changes the possible range of \(Y\).

Conditioning on \(X=0.60\) uses a density ratio. You cannot divide by \(P(X=0.60)\), which is zero for a continuous variable.

Problem 6

What can an alarm rate tell us?

Let \(F\) be the event that a machine has a fault, and \(A\) the event that its monitoring system raises an alarm. Treat the following as exact model probabilities, not sample estimates:

QuantityProbability
Alarm given a fault: \(P(A\mid F)\)0.80
Alarm given no fault: \(P(A\mid F^c)\)0.10
Alarm overall: \(P(A)\)0.24

(a) The fault probability \(r=P(F)\) is not given. Write a total-probability equation and use it to determine \(r\).

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · law of total probability
\[P(A)=P(A\mid F)\,P(F)+P(A\mid F^c)\,P(F^c)\]

\(F\) and \(F^c\) form a partition: exactly one of them happens. The overall alarm rate is a weighted average of the two conditional alarm rates, with weights \(r\) and \(1-r\). Here the equation is solved for the unknown weight.

Substitute
\[0.24=0.80r+0.10(1-r)=0.10+0.70r\quad\Longrightarrow\quad r=\frac{0.24-0.10}{0.70}=\mathbf{0.20}\]

This is algebra with stated model probabilities, not an estimate from a sample.

(b) If an alarm occurs, find the probability that the machine has a fault. Explain why this is not simply 0.80.

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · Bayes' theorem
\[P(F\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid F)\,P(F)}{P(A)}\]
Substitute
\[P(F\mid A)=\frac{(0.80)(0.20)}{0.24}=\frac{0.16}{0.24}=\frac23\approx\mathbf{0.6667}\]

\(P(A\mid F)=0.80\) is the fraction of faulty machines that alarm. \(P(F\mid A)\) is the fraction of alarms that come from faulty machines. The reference groups differ. In 100 machines: 20 are faulty and 16 of them alarm; 80 are healthy and 8 of them alarm. Of 24 alarms, 16 are real, so 16/24 = 2/3.

(c) An improved system reduces \(P(A\mid F^c)\) to 0.05 while leaving \(P(A\mid F)\) and the fault probability unchanged. Recalculate the probability of a fault given an alarm. Explain the direction of the change.

Show solutionHide solution
Formulas · total probability, then Bayes
\[P(A)=P(A\mid F)P(F)+P(A\mid F^c)P(F^c),\qquad P(F\mid A)=\frac{P(A\mid F)P(F)}{P(A)}\]
Substitute
\[P(A)_{\text{new}}=(0.80)(0.20)+(0.05)(0.80)=0.16+0.04=0.20\] \[P(F\mid A)_{\text{new}}=\frac{0.16}{0.20}=\mathbf{0.80}\]

The conditional fault probability rises from about 66.67% to 80%. True-fault alarms stay at 0.16, while false alarms fall from 0.08 to 0.04. A larger share of alarms now corresponds to genuine faults.

Problem 7

A corrected signal and repeated measurements

An instrument reports a raw reading \(R\) and a baseline reading \(B\), both in volts. Within one measurement pair:

QuantityMean (V)Standard deviation (V)
Raw reading \(R\)12.00.60
Baseline reading \(B\)5.00.80

The correlation between \(R\) and \(B\) within a pair is \(-0.25\). The baseline-corrected signal is \(Z=R-B\). Different measurement pairs are independent and identically distributed; \(R\) and \(B\) within the same pair are not assumed independent.

(a) Calculate \(\mathrm{Cov}(R,B)\), including its units.

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · covariance from correlation
\[\rho_{R,B}=\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(R,B)}{\sigma_R\,\sigma_B}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathrm{Cov}(R,B)=\rho_{R,B}\,\sigma_R\,\sigma_B\]
Substitute
\[\mathrm{Cov}(R,B)=(-0.25)(0.60)(0.80)=\mathbf{-0.12\ V^2}\]

Correlation is dimensionless; covariance carries the product of the variables' units, V × V = V².

(b) Find the mean, variance, and standard deviation of \(Z\). Explain why subtracting the baseline does not mean subtracting the two variances.

Show solutionHide solution
Formulas · linear combinations
\[E[aR+bB]=a\,E[R]+b\,E[B]\] \[\mathrm{Var}(aR+bB)=a^2\,\mathrm{Var}(R)+b^2\,\mathrm{Var}(B)+2ab\,\mathrm{Cov}(R,B)\] \[\text{For }Z=R-B\ (a=1,\ b=-1):\quad \mathrm{Var}(Z)=\mathrm{Var}(R)+\mathrm{Var}(B)-2\,\mathrm{Cov}(R,B)\]
Substitute
\[E[Z]=12.0-5.0=\mathbf{7.0\ V}\] \[\mathrm{Var}(Z)=0.60^2+0.80^2-2(-0.12)=0.36+0.64+0.24=\mathbf{1.24\ V^2}\] \[\sigma_Z=\sqrt{1.24}\approx\mathbf{1.1136\ V}\]

Variance uses squared coefficients, so the \(-1\) on \(B\) contributes \((-1)^2\mathrm{Var}(B)=+\mathrm{Var}(B)\). The covariance term keeps its sign through \(2ab=-2\). Here the covariance is negative, so it increases the variance of the difference: when \(R\) and \(B\) tend to move in opposite directions, subtracting \(B\) makes their deviations reinforce.

(c) The instrument averages the corrected signals from 16 independent measurement pairs. Find the mean and standard deviation of this average. Does the within-pair correlation invalidate the averaging rule?

Show solutionHide solution
Formula · mean of n i.i.d. variables
\[\bar{Z}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_i:\qquad E[\bar{Z}]=E[Z],\qquad \mathrm{Var}(\bar{Z})=\frac{\mathrm{Var}(Z)}{n},\qquad \sigma_{\bar{Z}}=\frac{\sigma_Z}{\sqrt{n}}\]
Substitute
\[E[\bar{Z}]=\mathbf{7.0\ V},\qquad \mathrm{Var}(\bar{Z})=\frac{1.24}{16}=0.0775\ \text{V}^2\] \[\sigma_{\bar{Z}}=\frac{\sqrt{1.24}}{\sqrt{16}}\approx\mathbf{0.2784\ V}\]

The rule is valid. It requires the 16 corrected signals \(Z_1,\dots,Z_{16}\) to be independent and identically distributed across pairs, which is given. The within-pair correlation of \(R\) and \(B\) is already included in \(\mathrm{Var}(Z)=1.24\); it does not create dependence between different pairs.
