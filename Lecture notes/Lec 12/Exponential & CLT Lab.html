One waiting time

Station 1 · Slides 3, 4, 6

A rate sets the time scale

T is the time from a chosen starting point until the next event, and T ~ Exp(λ). λ is a rate, in events per unit time. A larger rate means shorter waits.

fT(t) = λ e−λt,   t > 0
E[T] = 1λ    Var(T) = 1λ²    σT = 1λ
  • The curve starts at height λ and decays. Short waits are always the densest region, whatever the rate.
  • Height is density, not probability. With λ = 2 the curve starts at 2. Only areas are probabilities, and the total area is 1.
  • Units. λ is in time⁻¹, so 1/λ is a time and 1/λ² is a squared time.
  • Mean equals SD for every exponential. Doubling λ halves both and cuts the variance to one quarter.
The rate is not the mean wait. 15 particles per minute means a mean wait of 1/15 min = 4 s.

Station 2 · Slide 5

"By time t" and "after time t" split the probability

The CDF F(t) = 1 − e−λt answers "the event has happened by t". The tail P(T > t) = e−λt answers "we are still waiting at t". The two always add to 1.

P(T > t) = 1 − F(t) = 1 − (1 − e−λt) = e−λt
  • The tail is 1 − CDF. For the exponential the subtraction simplifies to e−λt, which is why the slide writes it that way.
  • The mistake to avoid is using the CDF itself for "more than t". In Example 11 that gives 0.7135 instead of 0.2865, the complement of the right answer.
  • Scaled time u = λt lets one pair of curves cover every rate.

Where the curves cross: the median

The curves can only be equal where each is 0.5. That point splits the probability in half, which is the definition of the median.

1
Set the tail equal to one half.
e−u = 0.5
2
Take ln of both sides.
−u = ln(0.5) ≈ −0.693
3
Multiply by −1. Since ln(0.5) = ln(1/2) = −ln 2, the signs cancel.
u = −ln(0.5) = ln 2 ≈ 0.693
4
Convert back to real time with u = λt.
tmedian = ln 2λ ≈ 0.693λ

ln(0.5) is the value of −u, not u. It is negative, and a scaled time can't be.

Why the median sits below the mean

Most of the probability is packed near zero, so the halfway point comes early. A long right tail of occasional very long waits pulls the mean up, because each long wait adds its full size to the average. Move λ: the ratio median/mean stays at 0.693 and P(T ≤ mean) stays at 63.2%.

At λ = 0.25 s⁻¹ (Example 11), the median wait is 2.77 s and the mean is 4 s. About 63% of waits are shorter than the average wait.

Station 3 · Slide 7

One process, two questions

A Poisson process with rate λ can be described by a count, N(t) ~ Poisson(λt), or by a wait, T ~ Exp(λ). "The first event comes after t" and "no events in [0, t]" are the same event.

{ T > t } = { N(t) = 0 }

Station 5 · Slides 10–11, 14

Memorylessness: the clock resets

If you have already waited s with no event, the chance of waiting at least t more is the same as if you had just started. Only the additional time t enters the answer.

P(T > s + t | T > s) = P(T > t)

Why it holds

1
Use the conditional-probability ratio. {T > s+t} is a subset of {T > s}, so the intersection is just {T > s+t}.
P(T > s+t | T > s) = P(T > s+t)P(T > s)
2
Insert the exponential tails. The factor e−λs cancels.
e−λ(s+t)e−λs = e−λt
The two events are nested, which is the opposite of independent. The cancellation comes from the subset relationship and the exponential form, not from an independence assumption.
Added: from class discussion

"The average is 4 s and I've waited 3 s. Shouldn't the next second be more likely?"

That intuition fits things with a schedule or wear. It does not fit an exponential wait, because the average of 4 s is not a schedule. Picture the process flipping a weighted coin in every tiny slice of time. After 300 slices of "no", the next slice has exactly the same chance. Nothing accumulates, so the event is never "due".

The average of 4 s covers all waits, and most of them are short. Waits that have already lasted 3 s are a selected group from the long tail, and that group runs longer than average. Conditioning on "I've waited 3 s" removes every short wait from the picture.

Website hits: condition, then take the complement

Hits arrive at 3 per minute, and T is in minutes. Part (1): P(more than one minute without a hit). Part (2): given two quiet minutes, P(a hit in the next minute).

(1)   P(T > 1) = e−3(1) = e−3 ≈ 0.0498
  • A full quiet minute is rare: 3 hits are expected every minute.
  • Part (2) asks about the interval from minute 2 to minute 3, given no earlier hit. A hit in the next minute is the complement of waiting a full additional minute.
Slide 14 trap: computing P(T ≤ 3) = 1 − e−9 ≈ 0.9999 ignores the condition. The elapsed two minutes do not add to the extra wait.

1
Map the problem onto the template: s = 2 min elapsed, t = 1 additional min, s + t = 3, λ = 3 per min.
2

P(T ≤ s+t | T > s) = P(T ≤ 3 | T > 2)
3

= 1 − P(T > 3 | T > 2)

Station 7 · Slides 15–16

Three layers: observation, sample, sampling distribution

The CLT is a statement about the distribution of X̄ (or Sn) across repeated samples. It does not make the population or the individual observations normal.

X̄ ≈ N(μ, σ²/n)    Sn ≈ N(nμ, nσ²)
  • Conditions: X1, …, Xn independent, identically distributed, with finite variance σ² > 0. "Any population" is shorthand that leaves these out.
  • The second slot is the variance, as everywhere in this course.
Added: from class discussion
LayerWhat it isExample 11 waitsShape
1 · XiOne observationOne wait, e.g. 2.3 sExponential, always
2 · X1, …, XnOne sample of size n40 waits recorded in one sessionLooks like the population (skewed)
3 · x̄1, x̄2, …Many samples, each reduced to one mean or sumRepeat the 40-wait session many timesApproximately normal for large n
  • X̄ is computed from one sample (layer 2), giving one number. N(μ, σ²/n) describes how that number would vary if you repeated the sampling (layer 3). With only one sample, it tells you how far your x̄ is likely to be from μ.
  • A bigger sample sharpens the picture of the population. It does not make that picture normal. Only the histogram of many sample means becomes normal.
  • If the population is normal, X̄ is exactly normal for every n. Nothing needs to converge.
Wording tip: say "a sample of n observations", not "n samples". The second phrasing is common, and it is exactly what causes the confusion between layers 2 and 3.

Station 8 · Slide 17

Use the SD of the variable in your event

One observation, a mean and a sum share the same μ and σ but have different spreads. A mean divides the spread by √n; a sum multiplies it by √n.

Z = X̄ − μσ/√n     Z = Sn − nμσ√n
  • Why √n for a sum, not n? Var(Sn) = nσ² under independence. Independent fluctuations partly cancel. A sum SD of nσ would only happen if the observations were perfectly correlated.
Added: from class discussion
  • Sn is the total of the n values within one sample. Repeat the sampling and you get many totals; their histogram is approximately N(nμ, nσ²).
  • The mean and the sum are the same variable on a different scale: Sn = n·X̄. So n·μ = nμ, and n·(σ/√n) = σ√n.

Example 11 waits again: μ = σ = 4 s.

Station 9 · Slides 18–19

How large is "large enough"?

The mean of n exponential waits has an exact distribution (a gamma). Compare it to the CLT normal curve. The bulk lines up quickly; the tails take much longer.

  • Slide 18: n = 1 shows strong right skew, n = 5 less skew with the same mean, n = 40 narrower and more symmetric. Individual waits stay exponential throughout.
  • Skewness fades slowly. The exponential has skewness 2, and the skewness of X̄ is 2/√n: about 0.89 at n = 5 and 0.32 at n = 40.
  • Check the tail you are asked about. The normal curve is least accurate far from the centre. A skewed population puts too little probability in the normal's left tail and too much in its right.
  • n > 30 is a working guideline, not a guarantee. A nearly symmetric population can work with a smaller n.
A large n does not repair selection bias or justify an unexamined independence assumption.

Station 10 · Added: not in the slides

What the CLT approximates, and when you need it

The CLT replaces the true, often unknown, distribution of a mean or sum with a normal curve that has the right mean and variance. Then a z-table does the rest.

The problem it solves

To find P(total of 65 processing times is between 50 and 55 h) exactly, you would need (1) the exact distribution of one processing time, which Example 13 doesn't give, and (2) the distribution of a sum of 65 of them, which is hard even when (1) is known. The CLT skips both steps and needs only μ and σ.

Use it for

  • Totals: machine time, load on a structure, time to clear a job queue.
  • Averages: the mean of 40 sensor readings or 40 waiting times.
  • Counts: a binomial count is a sum of 0/1 trials (Station 11); a Poisson count is a sum of many small independent pieces (Station 12).
  • Later in the course: confidence intervals and hypothesis tests for means and proportions.

Don't use it for

  • One observation. P(a single wait > 5 s) uses the exponential. One wait is not normal, whatever n you collected.

Using a normal curve on a single skewed observation is off by more than 11 percentage points.

Station 11 · Slide 20

A binomial count is a sum, so the CLT applies

If X ~ Bin(n, p) with np > 10 and n(1 − p) > 10, then X ≈ N(np, np(1 − p)). Both checks keep the distribution from piling up against 0 or n.

Added: from class discussion

1

Station 12 · Slide 21

A Poisson count is a sum too

If X ~ Poisson(λ) with λ > 10, then X ≈ N(λ, λ). The mean and variance are both λ, so the SD is √λ. The slide corrects a source that wrote N(λ, λ²).

Notation: here λ is the mean count in the interval (λt in slide 7's notation), not the per-unit-time rate of Part A.
Added: from class discussion

Continuity correction: give each integer a width

A count puts probability on integers; the normal curve gives area. Treat each integer k as a bar from k − 0.5 to k + 0.5. Included endpoints keep their full bars, excluded ones lose them.

Discrete eventContinuous event
X ≤ kY < k + 0.5
X < kY < k − 0.5
X ≥ kY > k − 0.5
X > kY > k + 0.5
a ≤ X ≤ ba − 0.5 < Y < b + 0.5
a < X < ba + 0.5 < Y < b − 0.5
  • Easiest route: rewrite the event as an inclusive range of integers first (X < 10 becomes X ≤ 9), then add 0.5 outward on both ends.
  • < versus ≤ for Y doesn't matter, because Y is continuous.
  • Example 14: X ~ Bin(100, 0.5), so Y ~ N(50, 25) and σ = 5. 45 ≤ X ≤ 55 becomes 44.5 < Y < 55.5, z = ±1.10, area 0.7287.
  • The exact binomial answer is also about 0.7287. Without the correction, z = ±1 gives 0.6827, about 4.6 points too low.
Only integer counts get ±0.5. Machine-hours in Example 13 are continuous and get none.

Station 14 · Slides 24–28 · Example 13

Total processing time for 65 parts

Each part needs a Machine 1 operation (mean 0.40 h, SD 0.10 h) and a Machine 2 operation (mean 0.45 h, SD 0.15 h). Find P(50 < combined total < 55 h).

Worked solution

1
Define the sums. Xi and Yi are the machine-1 and machine-2 times for part i.
S1 = ΣXi,   S2 = ΣYi,   S = S1 + S2
2
Assume independent parts, the same moments for every part, and independent machine sequences. Then the moments are exact; only the normal shape is approximate.
3
Square each SD first, then multiply by 65.
S1 ≈ N(26.00, 0.6500),   S2 ≈ N(29.25, 1.4625)
4
Add means and add variances, then take the square root.
S ≈ N(55.25, 2.1125),   σS ≈ 1.4534 h
5
Standardize. No continuity correction: S is a continuous time.
zL ≈ −3.6121,   zU ≈ −0.1720
6
Take the difference of the left-tail areas.
Φ(−0.1720) − Φ(−3.6121) ≈ 0.4316

Plausibility: the whole interval lies below the mean of 55.25 h, so the answer must be under 0.5. The lower limit is 3.6 SDs away and removes almost nothing.

This is total machine-hours, not the clock time of a schedule where the two machines run in parallel.
