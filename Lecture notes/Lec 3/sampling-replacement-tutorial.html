Draw, Replace, Repeat

Put a marble back before the next draw, or don't — that one choice decides whether your draws stay independent, and whether it's the population size or the sample size that ends up mattering.

What to notice: the with-replacement urn's odds never move — every draw sees the exact same urn. The without-replacement urn's odds shift after every draw, because the urn itself is now a different urn. That drifting probability is exactly what "the draws aren't independent" looks like.
2

What "independence" is actually measuring

Two draws are independent when knowing the outcome of the first tells you nothing about the second. We can measure that directly as a correlation between draw 1 and draw 2. With replacement, it's always exactly zero. Without replacement, it depends on how big the population is.

Correlation between two draws — with replacement
0.000
Correlation between two draws — without replacement
−0.053

For a population of size N, that without-replacement correlation works out to exactly −1 ⁄ (N − 1). Slide N up toward a thousand and watch it crawl toward zero — a big enough population makes "without replacement" behave almost exactly like "with replacement."

3

It's the fraction sampled, not the sample size, that matters

A sample of 500 out of a million voters barely dents the population. A sample of 500 out of 600 students in a small program uses most of it up. Statisticians summarize the shrinkage in variance with the finite population correction, applied whenever you sample without replacement:

FPC = √[ (N − n) / (N − 1) ]

Rule-of-thumb readnear-independent
The common guideline: once your sample passes about 10% of the population, the correction starts to bite — the without-replacement variance visibly undercuts the with-replacement figure, and treating the draws as independent starts to overstate your uncertainty. Below 10%, the two are close enough that most textbooks just let you use the simpler with-replacement formulas.

✓

Takeaways

  • With replacement, every draw comes from an unchanged population — draws are exactly independent, and standard formulas apply directly.
  • Without replacement, each draw slightly reshapes the population left behind, so draws carry a small negative correlation of about −1⁄(N−1).
  • That correlation shrinks toward zero as the population grows — with a huge population, "without replacement" and "with replacement" are practically the same thing.
  • What actually controls how much it matters is the sampling fraction n⁄N, not n by itself. Past roughly 10% of the population, the finite population correction becomes too large to ignore.
