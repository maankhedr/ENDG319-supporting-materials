01 Choosing a display

What data do you have?

Before you plot anything, answer two questions. The display should match the data — not the other way around.

How many variables are you looking at?
How many observations do you have?
Stem-and-leaf plot or dotplot
preserves every exact value · shows gaps, clusters, and repeats
Histogram or boxplot
summarizes shape efficiently · individual values are no longer legible one by one
Scatterplot
shows the relationship between the two paired measurements
Note — categorical data (not quantitative) is summarised with a frequency table and a bar chart. Never a histogram.

03 Same data, different story

What a boxplot alone would never tell you

Sixty dormant-period measurements from the geyser Old Faithful, in minutes. All four displays below are built from the exact same 60 numbers, at the exact same time.

Stem-and-leaf shows the split

Dotplot shows the split

Histogram shows the split

Boxplot hides the split

The punchline: the histogram and dotplot both show two distinct clusters — short dormant periods around 50 minutes, longer ones around 75–85 minutes — with a visible gap between. The boxplot reports a perfectly ordinary median and IQR and shows none of it. A boxplot can suggest skew, but it cannot reveal gaps or multiple peaks — you'd walk away from the boxplot alone thinking this was an unremarkable, roughly single-peaked dataset.
04 Reading scatterplots

Four things to check in any scatterplot

Bivariate data pairs two quantitative measurements per observation. Every scatterplot can be described the same way — direction, form, strength, and unusual points.

Direction

Positive association

Cure time (hrs) vs. compressive strength (MPa). As x increases, y increases — an upward pattern.

Form

Curved, not linear

Reaction rate vs. temperature. The dashed line shows what a linear pattern would look like — the actual points curve away from it.

Unusual points

One point breaks the pattern

Every other point follows the trend line closely. One does not — a candidate outlier or high-leverage point worth a second look.

Remember: association can support prediction, but it does not establish causation.
05 Takeaways

Before you build the next graph

  • 01Match the display to the variable type, the sample size, and the question you're actually asking.
  • 02Stem-and-leaf plots and dotplots preserve exact values; histograms and boxplots trade exact values for a clearer sense of shape.
  • 03A boxplot is excellent for comparing groups — but it can hide gaps, clusters, and multiple peaks that a histogram or dotplot would show immediately.
  • 04A scatterplot is described by direction, form, strength, and unusual points — always in that order.
  • 05A good graph supports an interpretation. It does not replace one.
