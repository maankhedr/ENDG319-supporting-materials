01 · Sample spaces

Discrete vs. continuous sample spaces

A sample space can either be a list you could (in principle) count on your fingers, or an unbroken interval with infinitely many possible values in between. The type of sample space determines how you'll eventually assign probabilities to it.

discreteCoin & die outcomes

Outcomes can be listed one by one — there's a gap between each possible value, and nothing can fall between them.

S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}

Rolling a die gives exactly one of six separated values. You could point to each one individually:

Six dots, six gaps between them — a countable, finite sample space.

continuousMotor shaft temperature

An engineer measures the surface temperature of a running motor shaft. The thermometer can, in theory, report any real value in its calibrated range.

S = {t : 40 < t < 90 °C}

Slide the reading below — notice there's no "next" temperature after any given one; you can always find another reading in between.

02 · Enumerating compound outcomes

Tree diagrams, beyond the coin toss

A tree diagram enumerates every stage of an experiment systematically so nothing is missed and nothing is duplicated. Below are two examples that don't use a coin: one where every stage has the same number of branches (illustrating rm), and one where the stages have different branch counts.

Spinner, spun twice — equal branching

A three-colour spinner (Red, Green, Blue) is spun twice. Each stage has r = 3 results, over m = 2 stages, so the tree has rm = 9 terminal paths.

9 outcomes: RR, RG, RB, GR, GG, GB, BR, BG, BB

Supplier & inspection — unequal branching

A part is sourced from one of 3 suppliers, then inspected as Pass or Fail (2 results). Stage 1 and Stage 2 don't have the same number of branches — the tree still enumerates all 3 × 2 = 6 paths correctly.

6 outcomes: A-Pass, A-Fail, B-Pass, B-Fail, C-Pass, C-Fail

04 · Interpreting probability

Probability vs. observed relative frequency

P(rolling a 6) = 1/6 ≈ 0.167 is a fixed, theoretical number baked into a fair die. The relative frequency you actually observe after rolling wobbles around at first — but should settle in near 1/6 as you roll more. Press the button to roll ten more times and watch the running proportion converge.

05 · General addition rule

Why we subtract the intersection

Sets A and B below are each populated with five outcomes and their individual probabilities. One outcome — the one worth 0.04 — belongs to both sets, so it sits in the overlap. Watch what happens if you simply add P(A) and P(B) without correcting for it.

P(A) — sum A's five circles 0.05 + 0.08 + 0.06 + 0.07 + 0.04 = 0.30
P(B) — sum B's five circles 0.09 + 0.05 + 0.07 + 0.10 + 0.04 = 0.35
Naive direct sum (wrong) 0.30 + 0.35 = 0.65 — the 0.04 circle got counted twice, once inside P(A) and once inside P(B).
P(A ∩ B) — the shared circle 0.04
General addition rule P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A∩B)
= 0.30 + 0.35 − 0.04 = 0.61
Check, by counting unique circles directly 4 A-only (0.26) + 4 B-only (0.31) + 1 shared (0.04) = 0.61 ✓
06 · Side by side

Mutually exclusive vs. general addition

Same die, same "or" question — but whether you can add probabilities directly depends entirely on whether the two events overlap.

Disjoint events — direct addition

A = {1, 2}    B = {5, 6}

A ∩ B = ∅  →  no outcome is shared, so nothing is double-counted.

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B)
= 2/6 + 2/6 = 4/6 ≈ 0.667

This is the special case of the addition rule from slide 26 — it only works because A and B don't overlap.

Overlapping events — general rule

A = {2, 4, 6} (even)    B = {4, 5, 6} (> 3)

A ∩ B = {4, 6}  →  two outcomes are shared and would be counted twice.

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A∩B)
= 3/6 + 3/6 − 2/6 = 4/6 ≈ 0.667

This is slide 27's general rule. Skipping the subtraction here would (wrongly) give 6/6 = 1.0.
