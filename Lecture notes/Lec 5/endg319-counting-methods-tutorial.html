What makes two outcomes different?

Every counting problem comes down to that one question. Answer it correctly and the right formula — fundamental principle, permutation, or combination — falls out on its own.

Are AB and BA the same outcome, or two different ones?

They're different — swapping the letters changes the outcome. That's a permutation: 2 letters, 2 arrangements each.

Example 6 — Arranging vs. selecting-and-ordering

Take three distinct items, X, Y, Z. These two small trees are fully drawn (nothing condensed) so you can see every arrangement. The same branching pattern is what scales up to the runners and lifeguards from the slides — it just gets too wide to draw once n reaches 5.

A. Arrange all 3 items — 3! = 6

Scaled up: 5 people in a line → n = k = 5 → 5! = 120 orders.

B. Choose and order 2 of 3 items — ₃P₂ = 6

Scaled up: 5 lifeguards, 3 stations → n = 5, k = 3 → ₅P₃ = 5 × 4 × 3 = 60 assignments.

Method 3Unordered groups — why we divide by k!

Same 6 leaves, grouped by combination

Reuse tree B from above. Every pair of leaves that contains the same two letters — just in a different order — collapses into one combination once order stops mattering. That's exactly why ₙCₖ = ₙPₖ / k!.

{X, Y} — from XY, YX {X, Z} — from XZ, ZX {Y, Z} — from YZ, ZY

6 ordered arrangements ÷ 2! orderings per group = 3 distinct combinations. Scaled up: choosing 5 identical door prizes for 5 of 30 guests → ₃₀C₅ = 142,506 groups (no ×5!, because the prizes aren't distinguishable).

Exercise

Choose a method

Each scenario below maps to exactly one of the four counting methods. Decide first, then reveal the answer.

Scenario A
A password is made of 8 lowercase letters. Any letter may be reused at any position.
Fundamental counting principle
26⁸ = 208,827,064,576

One outcome is a sequence of 8 independent stages, each with 26 choices — and repetition is explicitly allowed, so no stage ever loses an option.

Scenario B
6 finalists in a photography contest are ranked 1st through 6th, with no ties.
All-object permutation
6! = 720

Every one of the 6 finalists gets a distinct rank, and all 6 are placed — order matters, nothing repeats, and every object is used exactly once.

Scenario C
A starting lineup needs 5 named positions (PG, SG, SF, PF, C) filled from a 12-player roster. No player can fill two positions.
Permutation of k of n
₁₂P₅ = 12 × 11 × 10 × 9 × 8 = 95,040

Only 5 of the 12 players are chosen, and the 5 named positions make the selection ordered — swapping who plays PG vs. C is a different lineup.

Scenario D
A study group of 4 students is formed from a class of 20. No roles — everyone in the group is equal.
Combination
₂₀C₄ = 4,845

Only membership matters, not any ordering within the group — swapping which student is "listed first" doesn't create a new group.

Exercise

Find the counting error

Each approach below is incorrect. Try to spot the mistake before revealing the explanation and the corrected expression.

A. Computer configurations
A build needs one choice from each of 4 categories: 3 hard drives, 4 memory options, 2 video cards, 3 monitors.
3 + 4 + 2 + 3
Addition would only make sense if you were choosing from just one category. Since a complete build needs one option from every category at once, the choices combine multiplicatively — the fundamental counting principle, not addition.
3 × 4 × 2 × 3 = 72
B. Medal assignments
5 runners compete; gold, silver, and bronze go to 3 of them.
5 × 5 × 5
This treats every medal position as an independent draw from all 5 runners, which would let one runner win two or three medals. A runner can only fill one position, so after gold is awarded only 4 runners remain for silver, then 3 for bronze.
5 × 4 × 3 = 60 (₅P₃)
C. Identical-prize groups
30 people attend an event; 5 different people receive identical door prizes.
(30 choose 5) × 5!
Multiplying by 5! counts each group of 5 winners once for every order they could be announced in. But the prizes are identical — there's no "1st prize" vs. "5th prize" — so different announcement orders describe the exact same outcome and shouldn't be counted separately.
30 choose 5 = 142,506 (no × 5!)
D. All four bulbs good
A box has 8 good bulbs and 2 burned-out bulbs; 4 bulbs are drawn at random.
P(A) = 8 / 10
8/10 is the fraction of individual bulbs that are good — but the event asks about a group of 4 bulbs drawn together, not one bulb. The numerator and denominator both need to count 4-bulb subsets, using the same convention.
(8 choose 4) / (10 choose 4) = 70/210 = 1/3
Recap

One outcome, one method

Whatever the scenario, describe one complete outcome first — the method follows automatically.

For probability with equally likely outcomes: P(A) = favourable count / total count — always count the same kind of outcome on both sides of the fraction.
