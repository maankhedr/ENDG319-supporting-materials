Station 1 · Slides 3–4

Read N(μ, σ²) correctly

μ moves the curve. σ stretches it. The second slot of the notation holds the variance σ², so you take a square root before standardizing.

f(x) = 1σ√(2π) exp[ −(x − μ)²2σ² ]
  • Peak at x = μ. The exponent is 0 there, and e⁰ = 1, so the height is 1/(σ√(2π)) ≈ 0.399/σ.
  • Symmetric about μ, so μ is both the mean and the median: half the area sits on each side.
  • Height is not probability. Shrink σ below about 0.4 and the peak passes 1. That is allowed because only areas are probabilities.
  • P(X = x) = 0. Shrink the red interval to width zero and its area goes to zero, even though the curve's height there stays put.
If a problem says N(10, 4), then σ = 2, not 4.

Station 2 · Slide 5

The 68–95–99.7 rule is a model statement

Under a normal model about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of the population lies within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of μ. A finite sample only comes close, and the gap shrinks as n grows.

  • Exact model values: Φ(1) − Φ(−1) = 0.6827, Φ(2) − Φ(−2) = 0.9545, Φ(3) − Φ(−3) = 0.9973.
  • Use the rule as a quick sanity check. A reading 3σ from the mean happens about 3 times in 1000.
  • Draw a few small samples. With n = 20, the fraction inside ±1σ regularly lands anywhere from about 50% to 85%.
The intervals are centred at μ and measured in standard deviations. The axis below is labelled that way so it fits any normal model.

Station 3 · Slides 6–8 · Example 7

Standard units go both ways

Aluminum sheet thickness X ~ N(10, 1.3²), in thousandths of an inch. A z-score counts signed standard deviations from μ and carries no units.

z = x − μσ   ⇔   x = μ + σz
  • Predict the sign first. A value above the mean gives z > 0; z < 0 gives a value below the mean.
  • Standardizing is just a relabelled ruler. Each tick on the top line sits directly over its z on the bottom line.
  • The normal assumption comes first. Standardizing a skewed variable gives a skewed variable with mean 0 and SD 1.

Finding z for a given percentile: linear interpolation

For a percentile you know the area p and want the location z. That is the table read backwards: search the body for p, then read z from the row and column labels. Usually p falls between two entries, as it does for the value in the box above.

1
Find the two neighbouring entries with Φ(z1) ≤ p ≤ Φ(z2), where z2 = z1 + 0.01.
2
Work out how far p sits between the two areas, as a fraction from 0 to 1.
fraction = p − Φ(z1)Φ(z2) − Φ(z1)
3
Move the same fraction of the 0.01 step along z.
zp ≈ z1 + fraction × 0.01
  • Worked example, p = 0.95: Φ(1.64) = 0.9495 and Φ(1.65) = 0.9505. The fraction is (0.9500 − 0.9495)/(0.9505 − 0.9495) = 0.5, so z ≈ 1.645. Software gives 1.6449. Here the nearest-entry rule can't choose, which is why 1.645 is the value everyone quotes.
  • Example 9(b), p = 0.75: Φ(0.67) = 0.7486 and Φ(0.68) = 0.7517. The fraction is 0.0014/0.0031 ≈ 0.45, so z ≈ 0.6745.
  • Why a straight line is good enough. Over a 0.01 step the Φ curve is almost straight, so the interpolation error is smaller than the table's own four-decimal rounding.
  • Below the median. For p < 0.5 use the negative half, or use symmetry: zp = −z1−p. For example z0.05 = −1.645.
  • Interpolate or take the nearest entry? The nearest entry (two decimals) is usually accepted. Interpolate when the question asks for more precision or p sits close to halfway between two entries.
  • Then restore the units if the question is about a measurement: xp = μ + σzp (Station 5).

A percentile is a location, not an area

The 75th percentile is the z with 0.75 of the area to its left. You look for 0.75 inside the table body, then read the z from the edges. Then restore the original units with x = μ + σz.

Φ(zp) = p  ⇒  xp = μ + σ zp
  • Nearest entry is usually accepted on exams.
  • Linear interpolation works well because over a 0.01 step the Φ curve is almost a straight line. Its error is smaller than the table's own rounding.

Station 6 · Slides 21–23 · Example 10

A linear change of scale keeps the curve normal

If X ~ N(μ, σ²) and Y = aX + b with a ≠ 0, then Y ~ N(aμ + b, a²σ²). The shift b moves the centre and leaves the spread alone.

meanμY = aμ + bkeep the sign of a, add b
varianceσY² = a²σ²b disappears
SDσY = |a|σspread is never negative
  • Example 10: C ~ N(40, 1²) in °C and F = 1.8C + 32. So F ~ N(104, 3.24): mean 104 °F, variance 3.24 (°F)², SD 1.8 °F.
  • A negative a flips the curve. Watch where μ + σ lands: it moves to the left of the new mean.
  • Standardizing is a linear transformation with a = 1/σ and b = −μ/σ. That is why Z ~ N(0, 1), and why X must be normal first.
Slide 22: before calculating, which quantity changes by adding 32?
Only the mean. Adding 32 slides every value by the same amount, so the distances between values (the spread) do not change. The variance and SD come from the 1.8 alone.

Station 7 · Slides 24–25

Variances add, even for a difference

For independent normal X and Y, X − Y ~ N(μX − μY, σX² + σY²). The coefficient on Y is −1, and (−1)² = +1.

Added example, not in the slides

Shaft in a bore. A shaft has diameter D ~ N(25.000, σD²) mm and the bore it slides into has diameter B ~ N(μB, σB²) mm, made independently. The clearance is C = B − D. The parts jam when C < 0.

W = Σ ciXi ~ N( Σ ciμi, Σ ci²σi² )
  • Here cB = +1 and cD = −1, so μC = μB − 25 and σC² = σB² + σD².
  • Why add? Two sources of variation can't cancel each other; either one can push the clearance up or down.
  • The red dashed curve shows what subtracting the variances would claim: a much narrower clearance and a jam rate that is far too optimistic.
  • Independence is the stated condition. Correlated parts would need a covariance term.

Station 8 · Slides 19–20, 26

One sheet versus the average of n sheets

For iid Xi ~ N(μ, σ²), the sample mean is exactly normal: X̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n). Standardize with the SD of the variable in the question: σ for one observation, σ/√n for a mean.

Where σ²/n comes from

1
Distribute the 1/n in the definition of the sample mean (slide 19).
X̄ = 1nX1 + ⋯ + 1nXn
2
That is a linear combination (slide 24) with every ci = 1/n. Independence lets the variances add.
3
Identical distributions make every μi = μ and σi² = σ².
mean = n·1nμ = μ    var = n·1n²σ² = σ²n
  • No large-sample approximation needed because the data are already normal. Without normality, X̄ is only approximately normal for large n (Central Limit Theorem).
  • SE shrinks like 1/√n. Quadrupling n halves the SE.
  • When σ is unknown, the estimated SE is s/√n. An estimate is not the parameter.

Station 9 · Slide 27

Is a normal model reasonable?

A histogram gives a rough impression. A normal probability plot (Q–Q plot, §4.10) is sharper: if the data are normal, the points fall close to the straight line.

  • How the Q–Q plot is built. Sort the data and standardize it. Pair the i-th smallest value with the normal quantile z at cumulative area (i − 0.5)/n. Normal data line up along y = x.
  • Right skew bends the points upward at the right end: the large values are too large for a normal model.
  • Small samples wander even when the data are normal. Draw several normal samples at n = 15 to see how much. Not seeing outliers doesn't prove normality; use what you know about the process.
  • One outlier shows up as a single point far off the line. Investigate it before modelling. On its own it neither proves non-normality nor justifies a lognormal model.

Station 10 · Slides 28–32

Lognormal: normal after taking logarithms

If X ~ N(μ, σ²) then Y = eX is lognormal and ln Y = X is normal. The parameters μ and σ² describe ln Y, not Y.

fY(y) = 1σ y√(2π) exp[ −(ln y − μ)²2σ² ],  y > 0
P(Y ≤ y) = Φ( ln y − μσ )
  • The 1/y factor comes from the change of variables (d ln y / dy = 1/y) and keeps the total area at 1.
  • The two shaded areas are the same number. ln is increasing, so Y ≤ y exactly when ln Y ≤ ln y.
  • Median eμ < mean eμ + σ²/2. That gap is right skew in numbers. The mean of Y is not μ, and not even eμ.
  • Lognormal models fit positive quantities built from multiplicative effects: particle sizes, permeabilities, concentrations, failure times.

Since Yr = erX, E[Yr] is the normal moment-generating function at r: E[erX] = exp(rμ + r²σ²/2). r = 1 gives E[Y] = eμ+σ²/2. r = 2 gives E[Y²] = e2μ+2σ². Var(Y) = E[Y²] − (E[Y])² = e2μ+2σ² − e2μ+σ² = (eσ² − 1)e2μ+σ².
