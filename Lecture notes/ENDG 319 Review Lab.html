Block 1 · L3–L4 · 5 minutes

Quartiles by the course convention, and what an outlier moves

Edit the data or swap the 40 for a 400. The steps show the (n+1) position rule; the readouts show which summaries react to the extreme value and which ignore it.

Position:  Lp = p100(n + 1)
Fences:  Q1 − 1.5 × IQR  and  Q3 + 1.5 × IQR
  • Whole-number position: use that sorted value. Otherwise average the two neighbours. Excel and NumPy use other rules, so their answers differ.
  • Median and IQR resist outliers. Mean, SD and range do not.
  • Whiskers do not stop at the fences. Each whisker ends at the most extreme data value still inside its fence; any value beyond a fence is plotted as a separate outlier point.
  • A long right tail pulls the mean above the median.
  • s² divides by n − 1: deviations are measured from x̄, which sits closer to the data than μ does, so dividing by n underestimates σ².
  • x̄, s, p̂ are statistics (computed). μ, σ, p are parameters (unknown).

Block 2 · L5–L6 · 12 minutes

A good test can still be wrong most of the time it fires

Each square is one of 1000 parts. Red squares are defective parts the inspection flags; gold squares are good parts it flags by mistake. Lower the defect rate and watch the gold outnumber the red, even though the test itself has not changed.

P(D | F) = P(F | D) P(D)P(F | D) P(D) + P(F | D′) P(D′)
  • P(A | B) makes B the new reference group. P(A | B) and P(B | A) are different numbers; mixing them is the most common error in the course.
  • Build the denominator first with total probability: every way the evidence can happen.
  • Counting: ask whether order changes the outcome. Distinct roles = permutation; an unlabeled group = combination (divide by k!).
  • Drawing without replacement makes successive draws dependent.
Independent and mutually exclusive are near opposites. If P(A), P(B) > 0 and A, B cannot both happen, then P(A ∩ B) = 0 ≠ P(A)P(B), so they are dependent.
Pairwise independence does not give mutual independence. The product rule must hold for every subset of the events.

Exercise 2b · Bayes

2% of parts are defective. The inspection flags 95% of defective parts and wrongly flags 4% of good parts. (These are the instrument's starting values.)

  1. What fraction of all parts are flagged?
  2. Given a part is flagged, what is the probability it is defective?
  3. Given a part passes, what is the probability it is defective?
  4. Why is (2) so far below 95%?
  1. Rule · total probability: P(F) = P(F | D)P(D) + P(F | D′)P(D′).P(F) = 0.95(0.02) + 0.04(0.98)= 0.019 + 0.0392= 0.0582
  2. Rule · Bayes: P(D | F) = P(F | D)P(D) / P(F).P(D | F) = 0.019 / 0.0582= 0.326

Block 3 · L7–L8 · 10 minutes

Variances add, even when the variables subtract

Shaft diameter X and bore diameter Y are independent normals; clearance C = Y − X. The red area is the fraction of assemblies that interfere. The dashed grey curve is what you get if you subtract the SDs, which hides almost all of the risk.

Var(aX + bY) = a²σX² + b²σY² + 2ab Cov(X, Y)
  • Means are always linear, with no independence needed. Variances need independence, or the covariance term.
  • With b = −1, b² = 1: Var(X − Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y).
  • Add variances, never SDs. SDs combine as root-sum-of-squares.
  • Var(2X) = 4σ², but Var(X₁ + X₂) = 2σ² for iid copies. Doubling one part is not adding two parts.
  • A PDF value is not a probability and can exceed 1. E[X²] ≠ (E[X])².
Cov = 0 does not mean independent. X uniform on {−1, 0, 1} and Y = X² have zero covariance, but Y is completely determined by X.

Numerical example · subtracting does not cancel spread

Give each variable two equally likely values, one SD below and one SD above its mean. Then X₁ (mean 10) has SD exactly σ₁ and X₂ (mean 6) has SD exactly σ₂. Z = X₁ − X₂ has four equally likely combinations, listed below.

X₁X₁ + σ₁X₁ − σ₁X₂X₂ + σ₂X₂ − σ₂Z = X₁ − X₂ (4 combinations)Max range of Z
For illustration only. The max range μZ ± (σ₁ + σ₂) only shows the most extreme combinations; it is not the SD of Z. Summing the standard deviations (3 + 4 = 7) is the wrong way to combine them, and subtracting them (4 − 3 = 1) is wrong too. The correct rule is to sum the variances and take the square root: SD(Z) = √(σ₁² + σ₂²) = √(3² + 4²) = 5 at the default values.

Block 5 · L11–L12 · 10 minutes

Memorylessness: only the additional wait matters

You have already waited s minutes. The gold area is P(T > s); the red area is P(T > s + t). Their ratio is the conditional probability, and it always equals P(T > t). The dashed red curve is the conditional density: the original curve, shifted to start at s.

P(T > s + t | T > s) = e−λ(s+t)e−λs = e−λt
  • λ is a rate (per minute); the mean wait is 1/λ. Larger rate, shorter wait.
  • "Wait longer than t" uses the tail e−λt, not the CDF 1 − e−λt.
  • The median ln 2/λ is below the mean; about 63% of waits are shorter than the mean wait.
  • N(μ, σ²) lists the variance: N(25, 0.04) means σ = 0.2.
  • Areas go x → z → Φ(z). Percentiles run backwards: area → z → x = μ + zσ.

Lognormal · the parameters live on the log scale

ln X ~ N(2, 0.5²). Find P(X > 10).

Wrong path
z = (10 − 2)/0.5 = 16  ⇒  P ≈ 0

Treats 2 and 0.5 as the mean and SD of X. They describe ln X.

Right path
z = (ln 10 − 2)/0.5 = 0.605  ⇒  P = 0.273

Move the cut-off to the log scale, then standardize. Median = eμ = 7.39; mean = eμ + σ²/2 = 8.37.

Block 6 · L12–L13 · 10 minutes

One item or a mean of n? Decide before you standardize

Fill volume has mean 500 mL and SD 10 mL. The blue curve is single bottles; the red curve is the mean of n bottles, which narrows as σ/√n. The same cut-off gives very different tail areas.

One item: z = x − μσ    Mean: z = x − μσ/√n
  • The CLT makes x̄ approximately normal even when X is not. If X is normal, x̄ is exactly normal for any n.
  • SD describes individual measurements; SE = σ/√n describes sample means. If a problem gives the SE, do not divide by √n again.
  • A 95% CI does not mean "95% chance μ is in this interval." μ is fixed; 95% of intervals built this way capture it.
  • Sample size n = (zσ/E)², always rounded up. One-sided 95% bounds use 1.645, not 1.96.

Normal N(μ, σ²)

symmetric bell
Good forMeasurement error, dimensions, fill volumes; any sum or mean of many small independent effects.

Assumptions

Exponential(λ)

waiting time
Good forTime until the next event in a Poisson process; lifetime of a part with a constant failure rate.
f(t) = λe−λt,   P(T ≤ t) = 1 − e−λt,   P(T > t) = e−λt

Assumptions

  • Memoryless: no wear-out, so time already waited does not change the remaining wait.

  • t ≥ 0.

Sampling and approximations

Distributions of statistics

Sample mean X̄ (CLT)

mean of n
Good forProbabilities about an average or total of n items, and the basis for confidence intervals.
X ≈ N(μ, σ²/n),   SE = σ/√n
Total ΣXi ≈ N(nμ, nσ²)
Assumptions
  • A random sample of independent, identically distributed (iid) observations.
  • Exactly normal if X is normal; otherwise approximately normal for large n. More skew needs a larger n.
  • Independence is what gives σ²/n. Dependent data make this SE wrong, and a larger n does not fix that.
