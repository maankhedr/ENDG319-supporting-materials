What a linear transformation is

Background for the lecture

Linear algebra studies vectors and the functions that move them around in the simplest possible way. A transformation T is linear if it respects addition and scaling:

T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v)  and  T(cu) = c T(u)

In the animation this shows up three ways: the origin never moves, grid lines stay straight and parallel, and they stay evenly spaced. Because the grid is rigidly tied together, you only need to know where the two basis arrows land. The red arrow î lands at (a, c) and the blue arrow ĵ lands at (b, d). Those landing spots are the two columns of the matrix, and every other point follows: (x, y) ↦ x·î′ + y·ĵ′.

The determinant ad − bc tells you how area changes. The shaded unit square turns into a parallelogram of area |det|. When the determinant is negative the plane has been flipped over, which is why the flag points the other way after Reflect. When it is zero the whole plane is squashed onto a line.

Keep one picture in mind for the rest of the page: a linear map moves every point by the same rule. A random variable is a whole cloud of possible values, so a linear map moves the whole cloud at once, and its centre and spread change in predictable ways.

The same idea on a number line

Slide 3 · Y = aX + b

A line has only one basis vector, so a linear map on it is just multiplication by a single number a. If |a| > 1 it stretches, if |a| < 1 it compresses, and a negative a flips the line over, just like a negative determinant. Adding b afterwards slides everything along. Strictly, aX + b is an affine map (linear, then shifted), and that is the version the slides use.

Below, X has mean 2 and SD 1. Each grey line connects one possible value of X (top) to where the map sends it (bottom). Parallel lines mean a pure shift; lines that cross mean a reflection.

Try the reflection preset and watch the SD readout: the lines cross, the curve flips, but its width does not change. Spread is a distance, and distances can't be negative, which is why the SD uses |a| and the variance uses a².

The mean of a combination is the combination of the means

Slide 4 · E[c₁X + c₂Y] = c₁E[X] + c₂E[Y]

Start with two random variables, each with its own distribution and its own mean. Then build a new variable from them, Z = c₁X + c₂Y. The rule on slide 4 says you can find the mean of Z without ever looking at its distribution: scale each mean by its coefficient, keeping the sign, and add.

Variances add, even for a difference

Slides 5–6 · Var(Σ cᵢXᵢ) = Σ cᵢ²σ²Xᵢ, for independent Xᵢ

Independence (slide 5) means that every event about X is independent of every event about Y. One matching probability is not enough. When it holds, the covariance terms vanish and variances simply add, with each coefficient squared. A minus sign becomes (−1)² = 1, so subtracting a noisy quantity still adds its noise.

Standard deviations combine like the sides of a right triangle, not like lengths laid end to end. This is the root-sum-square rule from tolerance stacking.

The sample mean is a random variable too

Slides 9–10 · E[X̄] = μ, σX̄ = σ/√n

The formulas need the i.i.d. model: observations are independent (none carries information about another) and identically distributed (all come from the same population). Sampling without replacement from a finite lot breaks independence slightly, which is harmless when the sample is a small fraction of the lot.

The average X̄ = (1/n)ΣXᵢ is a linear combination with every coefficient equal to 1/n. The mean rule gives n · (1/n)μ = μ. The variance rule squares each coefficient: n · (1/n²)σ² = σ²/n.

The simulator draws 2,000 samples of size n, averages each one, and plots the averages against the population they came from. Pick a population and drag n.

Three things to notice. The histogram stays centred on μ for every n. Its width falls like 1/√n, so going from 16 to 64 only halves it. And even for the skewed and two-peaked populations, the averages start to look bell-shaped as n grows. That last effect is the central limit theorem, which comes later in the course.

Example 19: average fill volume in a case

Slide 11 · 24 bottles, each with mean 2.013 L and SD 0.005 L

Your turn

Treating the 24 bottles as i.i.d., what is the standard deviation of the case average X̄?

Show the working
μX̄ = 2.013 L (averaging keeps the mean)
σX̄ = 0.005/√24 ≈ 0.001021 L, about a fifth of one bottle's SD

What if the bottles aren't independent?

Bottles in a case are usually filled one after another on the same line, so a drifting filler can push them all high or all low together. If every pair of bottles has correlation ρ, the covariance terms from slide 41 give

σX̄ = σ √[(1 + (n − 1)ρ) / n]

which reduces to σ/√n at ρ = 0. Drag ρ up and watch the curve flatten out: with correlated bottles, adding more of them stops helping, because the shared error doesn't average away. This is the warning on slide 9 made concrete.
