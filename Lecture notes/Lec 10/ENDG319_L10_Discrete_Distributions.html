Poisson · slides 4–9

Counting events in an opportunity

X counts events in a fixed opportunity: a span of time, a length of wire, an area, or a volume of liquid. Counting in an interval does not make a count Poisson automatically. It is a modelling assumption.

P(X = x) = e−λ λxx!,   x = 0, 1, 2, …
  • λ is a mean count, not a probability. It can be larger than 1.
  • Mean = variance = λ, and SD = √λ.
  • Scale λ to the opportunity: λ = (events per unit) × (number of units).

Why the Poisson matters

  1. Simplicity. It has one parameter, λ, instead of two (n and p). Each term is easy to compute by hand, and the mean and variance are both λ.
  2. Insight. When events are rare, only the average count np matters, not n and p separately. The Bin → Poisson panel below shows this.
  3. Modelling when n is unknown. For customer arrivals or radioactive decays there is no meaningful "number of trials", only a rate. Here the Poisson is a model in its own right, not just an approximation. In practice this is the most important reason.
Three checks. 1. What is the opportunity? 2. What is λ for that whole opportunity? 3. For "exactly x", plug in that x, never the mean.

Hypergeometric · slides 10–17

A finite sample drawn without replacement

A population of N items holds R successes and N − R failures. Draw n distinct items. X counts the successes in the sample: X ~ H(N, R, n).

P(X = x) = RxN−Rn−xNn
  • Choose x of the successes and n − x of the failures, then divide by all n-item samples.
  • Support: max(0, R + n − N) ≤ x ≤ min(n, R).
  • Mean = n·R/N, the same as a binomial with p = R/N.
  • Variance = n·(R/N)(1 − R/N)·(N − n)/(N − 1). The finite-population factor is below 1, so the spread is smaller than the binomial's.
Random is not independent. Each draw removes an item, so the chance of a success on the next draw depends on what came before. Use the population on the right to see it happen.

Binomial vs hypergeometric

Why the binomial is wrong here, and when it is close enough

The binomial needs independent trials with the same p. Drawing without replacement breaks independence: each draw removes an item and changes the chance for the next one. The gold dots on the chart above show what the binomial would predict anyway.

  • Same centre. Both have mean n·R/N, so the dots and bars balance at the same point.
  • Too wide. The binomial leaves out the factor (N − n)/(N − 1). In Example 4 its variance is 1.824 instead of the correct 1.489.
  • Impossible outcomes. The binomial allows 0 to n successes and ignores R. For the bulbs, Bin(4, 0.2) gives P(X = 3) = 0.0256 and P(X = 4) = 0.0016, but only 2 bulbs are burned out.
The 5% rule of thumb. When the sample is a small fraction of the population (n/N under about 5%), each draw changes p by very little. The draws are then nearly independent, (N − n)/(N − 1) is close to 1, and the binomial is a good approximation. Both examples above are far from this: the buildings sample 20% and the bulbs 40%.

See it converge: grow the population, keep the sample

The success fraction stays near R/N = 0.24, as in Example 4. Only the population size changes.

Negative binomial · slides 22–24

Stop at the r-th success

The same trials as before, but now you stop at success number r. r is fixed in advance. X, the total number of trials, is random: X ~ NB(r, p), with x = r, r + 1, …

P(X = x) = x − 1r − 1 pr(1 − p)x−r

Where the formula comes from

X = x means two things happen together. Each piece covers different trials.

1

Exactly r − 1 successes in the first x − 1 trials, in any order. This is a binomial question: a fixed number of independent trials, counting successes.

1a · Start from the binomial PMF

For n trials, the probability of exactly k successes. (We write k instead of the usual x, because x already means the total number of trials here.)

P(k successes) = nk pk(1 − p)n−k
1b · Substitute n = x − 1 and k = r − 1

The trials before the last one number x − 1, and they must hold r − 1 successes.

x − 1r − 1 pr−1(1 − p)(x−1)−(r−1)
1c · Simplify the failure exponent

(x − 1) − (r − 1) = x − 1 − r + 1 = x − r. This is the number of failures among the first x − 1 trials.

x − 1r − 1 pr−1(1 − p)x−r
2

Trial x is a success, the r-th one. Its probability is p.

3

Multiply. The two pieces involve different trials, and the trials are independent, so P(both) = P(step 1) × P(step 2):

x − 1r − 1 pr−1(1 − p)x−r × p = x − 1r − 1 pr(1 − p)x−r
  • Why not C(x, r)? It would choose r success positions from all x trials, so the r-th success could land before trial x. Fixing trial x as a success and arranging only the first x − 1 rules that out.
  • Why independence matters. The multiplication in step 3 needs independent trials. Drawing without replacement from a small population breaks it, and the formula no longer applies.
  • Special case r = 1. C(x − 1, 0) = 1, which leaves (1 − p)x−1p: the geometric PMF.
  • Trial x must be the r-th success. So the first x − 1 trials hold exactly r − 1 successes, in any order.
  • X is the sum of r independent geometric waits, so mean = r/p and variance = r(1 − p)/p².
  • With r = 1 everything reduces to the geometric.

Connecting the models

Do these models assume sampling with replacement?

Not exactly. The binomial, geometric, negative binomial and multinomial assume independent trials with the same probabilities. Sampling with replacement is one way to get that, but it is not the assumption itself. These models describe a stream of trials and do not refer to a population at all. Replacement only matters when the trials are draws from a finite pool.

Three settings where independent trials hold

1

A process with no pool to deplete. Coin flips, a machine producing parts, network packets, free throws. Nothing is used up, so replacement is not a question.

2

Draws from a finite pool, with replacement. Returning each item restores the pool, so p stays constant and the draws are independent.

3

Draws from a pool much larger than the sample. Removing a few items barely changes p, so the model is a good approximation. This is why Example 5 specifies "a large industrial firm": auditing a few invoices out of thousands leaves the 20% essentially unchanged.

If you wait for successes while drawing without replacement from a small pool, such as the 10-bulb box, the geometric and negative binomial do not apply. The correct model is the negative hypergeometric, which is not covered in this lecture.

Multinomial · slides 26–30

Each trial lands in one of k categories

n independent trials. Each lands in exactly one of k categories with probabilities p₁, …, pk that add to 1. The result is a vector of counts that must add to n.

P(X₁=x₁, …, Xk=xk) = n!x₁!⋯xk! p₁x₁⋯pkxk

Where the coefficient comes from

The fraction counts the distinct sequences that give these totals. It does this in two moves: count every ordering, then remove the duplicates. The numbers follow the cell selected on the right.

1

n! counts every ordering, treating each item as distinct.

2

Dividing by x₁! ⋯ xk! removes orderings that look the same. Items in the same category are interchangeable, so shuffling them among themselves gives the same sequence.

3

Multiply by the probability of one sequence. The trials are independent, so every sequence with these counts has the same probability p₁x₁⋯pkxk.

  • With k = 2 categories, n!/(x!(n − x)!) is exactly C(n, x). The binomial coefficient is the special case, built on the same logic.
  • The same count, one category at a time:
  • Zero-count categories are harmless. 0! = 1 and p0 = 1, so they change nothing but still belong in the formula.
  • The fraction counts the orderings that produce the same totals.
  • Each count alone is binomial: Xi ~ Bin(n, pi).
  • The counts are not independent. They must sum to n, so once k − 1 counts are known the last one is fixed. Multiplying the binomial marginals gives the wrong joint probability.
  • Zero-count categories still belong in the formula (0! = 1, p⁰ = 1).

Second example · from binomial to multinomial

Tossing a die: one binomial, six binomials, one multinomial

Toss a fair die n times. We build up in three stages: first track only the sixes, then track every face with its own binomial, then combine the six counts into one multinomial.

1

One binomial: count only the sixes

Call a toss a success if it shows 6. Each toss is independent with p = 1/6, so the number of sixes is X₆ ~ Bin(n, 1/6). The other five faces are lumped together as "not a six".

2

Six binomials: one for each face

Nothing is special about 6. Do the same for every face: X₁ counts ones, X₂ counts twos, and so on. Each one alone is Bin(n, 1/6). Use + and − to set the counts, or toss again.

3

Combine them: the multinomial

The six counts describe one experiment, so they must be handled together. The multinomial gives the probability of the whole vector (x₁, …, x₆) at once:

Common mistakes (slide 33)

PoissonUsing a per-unit rate as λ instead of the mean for the whole opportunity.
HypergeometricTreating draws without replacement as independent, or ignoring the support.
GeometricCounting only the failures, although X includes the final successful trial.
Negative binomialUsing C(x, r) instead of fixing success r at trial x.
MultinomialDropping a zero-count category, or assuming the category counts are independent.
