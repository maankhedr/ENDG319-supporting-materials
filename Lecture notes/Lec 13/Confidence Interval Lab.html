Section 1 · Slides 3–4

Individual spread vs. uncertainty in the mean

Both plots share one cm axis. The top shows individual pistons, which spread with σ. The bottom collects sample means, which spread with σ/√n. Draw some samples and watch the bottom histogram form inside a much narrower curve.

σ (or s)  →  σX = σ√n  →  SE = s√n
  • σ describes one measurement: how far a single piston tends to be from μ.
  • σ/√n describes the average of n: how far X tends to be from μ. It comes from Var(X) = σ²/n for iid observations.
  • All three are in cm. Units do not tell you which one a problem gave you; the wording does.
If a problem already gives the standard error of the mean, do not divide by √n again.
"The mean of 100 pistons is 14.0 cm, with standard error 0.1 cm."
No.0.1 already describes X. Use SE = 0.1 (Example 1).
"100 boxes: mean 12.05 oz, sample standard deviation 0.1 oz."
Yes.s describes single boxes. SE = 0.1/√100 = 0.01 oz (Example 2-1).

Section 2 · Slides 6–8

The middle 95% describes possible sample means

This curve is the sampling distribution of X for the piston case: centred at μ = 14.0 with SE = 0.1 cm. The shaded band is where the middle (1 − α) of sample means land. Drop sample means and count how many land inside.

P(μ − 1.96σX ≤ X ≤ μ + 1.96σX) ≈ 0.95
  • E[X] = μ: the curve is centred on the truth.
  • Spread σ/√n: here 1/√100 = 0.1 cm.
  • Shape: exactly normal if pistons are normal, otherwise approximately normal by the CLT for large n (slide 7).
μ is fixed and unknown. Here the probability describes X, which is the quantity that varies from sample to sample.
Conditions first (slide 7): a random, representative sample and independent observations. A large n cannot fix a biased or dependent design. Section 5 lets you break independence.

Section 3 · Slide 9

The same distance, read in two directions

Move x̄. The top row asks whether x̄ is close to μ. The bottom row asks whether the interval around x̄ reaches μ. The two answers always agree, because both measure the same distance |x̄ − μ| against the same margin M = 1.96 × 0.1 = 0.196 cm.

|X − μ| ≤ 1.96σX

is equivalent to

X − 1.96σX ≤ μ ≤ X + 1.96σX
  • We cannot see μ, so we cannot draw the top band in practice.
  • We can see x̄, so we draw the bottom interval instead. It contains μ exactly when x̄ landed inside the top band.

Section 6 · Slides 13, 23–24

Random interval before sampling, fixed interval afterwards

Before sampling, the endpoints L and U are random variables: functions of a sample not yet drawn. After sampling they become two numbers, l and u. The 95% belongs to the procedure, so once the numbers exist you report confidence, not probability.

Before sampling

L = X − 0.196,   U = X + 0.196
L ∈ [μ − 0.392, μ]U ∈ [μ, μ + 0.392]interval misses μ

L ~ N(μ − 0.196, 0.1²) and U ~ N(μ + 0.196, 0.1²), but they are not independent: both come from the same X, so U = L + 0.392 for every sample. Each draw drops a linked pair onto the curves and a row below. The two curves don't add, so their crossing at μ has no meaning.

The capture event is the shaded middle 95% of either curve: L ≤ μ ≤ U ⟺ μ − 0.392 ≤ L ≤ μ ⟺ μ ≤ U ≤ μ + 0.392, so P(L ≤ μ ≤ U) = P(|X − μ| ≤ 0.196) = 0.95.

After the sample is observed

(l, u) = (13.804, 14.196)

A correct report: "We are 90% confident that the population mean diameter is between 14.73 and 14.91 cm." It also says nothing about 90% of individual rods. Section 6b · Slides 23–24 · Example 4

Same numbers, different meaning: what the 90% is attached to

A 90% confidence interval for the mean diameter of steel rods is (14.73, 14.91) cm. The two statements below use the same limits, but each attaches the 90% to something different. Pictures A to C show what each reading would mean.

False (frequentist) "The probability that the mean diameter is between 14.73 and 14.91 cm is 90%." The 90% is attached to μ, as if μ were a random quantity that falls inside these fixed limits 90% of the time. See picture A.
Correct "We are 90% confident that the population mean diameter is between 14.73 and 14.91 cm." The 90% is attached to the method that produced 14.73 and 14.91: in repeated use, 90% of the intervals it builds contain μ. See picture B.
Picture A

What the false statement assumes, and what is really there

The top row is what "probability 90%" would require: μ would need to have a distribution, so that 90% of its area sits between the limits. In this course, μ is a fixed constant. It has no distribution.

The bottom row is the real situation: one fixed interval and one fixed μ. We don't know where μ is, so the slider lets you put it anywhere.

P(14.73 ≤ μ ≤ 14.91), given this μ
Wherever μ is, this probability is 1 or 0, never 0.9. We only don't know which of the two it is. That lack of knowledge is what "confident" expresses.
Picture B

What "90% confident" refers to: the method, used again and again

The gold row is our interval, (14.73, 14.91), which is fixed. The rows below are what the same procedure would give on new samples of rods from a process whose true mean is the μ you set in picture A.

  • Our interval either contains μ or doesn't (yes/no).
  • The method captures μ about 90% of the time wherever μ is. That rate is the "90%" in the correct statement.

Move μ outside (14.73, 14.91) in picture A. Our gold interval now misses, but the method's rate stays near 90%. The two quantities are separate.

What planning controls

Sample-size planning runs the usual problem in reverse. You decide before collecting data how precise the interval must be, then solve for how much data that takes. A smaller d means more precision, which needs a larger n.

Quantity in M = zα/2σ/√nRole in planning
dYou choose it: the required precision
zα/2You choose it, through the confidence level (not the interval, which exists only after the data)
σNot a choice. It is a property of the process, so you plug in a planning estimate from a pilot study, past data or judgement
nThe unknown you solve for, and the one lever the study design controls
Piston example. Want 95% confidence (z = 1.96) and a margin of ±0.1 cm, and expect σ ≈ 1 cm: n ≥ (1.96 × 1 / 0.1)² = 384.16, so measure 385 pistons. Halving d to 0.05 multiplies the required n by four (Section 10).
Planned, not guaranteed

Section 12 · Slides 29–32

One-sided confidence bounds

When only one direction matters (a minimum mean strength, a maximum mean fill), put all of α in one tail. The cutoff becomes zα instead of zα/2, and the result is a ray instead of a segment. The ghost segment is the two-sided interval at the same level, for comparison.

Lower: L = x̄ − zαSE,  [L, ∞)
Upper: U = x̄ + zαSE,  (−∞, U]
Φ(zα) = 1 − α
  • A 95% one-sided bound uses 1.645, the same multiplier as a 90% two-sided interval. The 95% lower bound equals the lower end of the 90% interval.
  • At the same level, a one-sided bound sits closer to x̄ than the matching two-sided endpoint.
My addition, not on the slides
Putting Example 2-2's bounds together, [12.0336, 12.0733] oz, gives an exact 94% two-sided (asymmetric) interval: the two ways to miss are disjoint, so 0.05 + 0.01 = 0.06.

Combining the bounds is my addition, not from the slides
Combined [L, U]: μ can be missed by falling below L (risk αL) or above U (risk αU), never both at once, because L < U for every sample. The risks add, so the coverage is 1 − αL − αU: . That is neither of the two stated levels.
